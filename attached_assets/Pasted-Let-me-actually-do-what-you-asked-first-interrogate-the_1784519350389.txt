And once you look past the fancy-sounding origin domain, the actual test reveals something worse: this may be a near-tautological finding, not a real one. The hypothesis is framed around "collateral sensitivity in resistance rotation" — a real, specific pharmacology concept about a drug sequencing strategy. But what actually got tested is just: does the indication name contain the word "refractory" or "resistant." Think about what that word almost definitionally means: an indication described as "refractory" is, by construction, a later-line treatment setting — you cannot be refractory to a first-line drug that hasn't been tried yet. So "indication name contains 'refractory'" is close to a linguistic proxy for "this is not this drug's original approval" — which is uncomfortably close to the exact same label-confounding problem the architect's hard-block was built to catch a few sessions ago ("measured mechanistic connectivity predicts success" got correctly discarded for this reason). The three confounds tested — oncology concentration, prior repurposing momentum, established-product incumbency — are all real and properly checked, but none of them directly test this specific concern: that the word itself may partially encode the outcome by definition, not by biology.

A real, properly-run statistical test doesn't produce NaN even when a result is unremarkable; it produces a real, if boring, number.
